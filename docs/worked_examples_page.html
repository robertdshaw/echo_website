A distressed credit fund, sovereign and state oil paper

The month the families
started leaving

[[ROB: photograph credit]]

A carrier group off the coast, a capital that had cried wolf before, and a client holding paper that would be worth very different things depending on who ran the country in March.

Four weeks of monitoring, November to December 2025. Delivered through a London-based advisory partner.

The question the client brought was not whether the government would survive. It was who would be on the other side of a contract in six months, and whether that person would recognise the paper.

Through the second half of November the same pattern kept arriving from sources that do not usually agree and were not talking to each other. Families of senior officials leaving through the western border. Ministries destroying records. Senior figures preparing fallback positions in the south. None of it would have cleared our bar alone. Together, from independent channels, it did.

What mattered was the gap between the readings. External pressure was climbing and jumped again in the last week of November. Elite cohesion had been falling all autumn and turned up off its low at the start of December. The military reading went the other way entirely and sat at its lowest point on the day we made the call.

The judgement was that the military line could not hold against the other two, and that when it went it would go quickly. That is why the call was a rupture rather than a slow decline.

Thirty two days later the president was taken. The successor came from inside the ruling coalition, the armed forces stayed intact, and the client's contract and counterparty assumptions held.

We got the mechanism wrong. We weighted a negotiated exit above a strike, and the strike came first. The questions were written about outcomes rather than about how, which is why they resolved anyway. That is a design choice, not luck.

Consensus in the press was collapse or the opposition taking power. The client positioned for succession inside the existing structure.

The same desk, six months on

Two gates, and the market
was watching the wrong one

[[ROB: photograph credit]]

Oil licensing news kept moving the bonds. It should not have. The authorisation that lets crude leave the country and the one that lets a US holder be paid are different pieces of paper with different timetables.

Country brief, May 2026. Delivered through a London-based advisory partner.

Every few weeks a licensing headline arrived and the paper moved with it. The reasoning was intuitive and wrong: more oil means more money means the bonds get paid.

The first authorisation governs production and export. Output can recover under it while the bonds stay exactly where they are. The second governs secondary trading and the payment of coupons and principal to US holders. That is the one that releases the paper, and in May it had not moved.

The same brief found a second error in the same direction. The export figure being quoted was gross. Four deductions come off before anything reaches the state: cost recovery under the operating licences, an administration fee on sanctioned-channel sales, royalties paid in kind rather than cash, and the largest operator's dollar revenue staying offshore by design. Worked through the licence terms, roughly half the headline was left.

So a capacity-to-pay model built on the export print overstated the position by close to half, and a catalyst calendar built on licensing news was pointed at the wrong gate.

Both errors came from reading a headline instead of a document. Neither required a source. They required someone to sit down with the licence terms and do the arithmetic.

The catalyst calendar was rebuilt around the second gate, with the multilateral capacity assessment as the step that had to come before either could be priced.

A fund with no position, and a claimant with unpaid awards

Where the money stops

[[ROB: photograph credit]]

A court-run sale, a public queue of creditors, and a question that sounded like one decision but was really four.

Scoping work, September 2026, and a deep-dive assessment, April 2026.

The fund's question was whether to enter the queue. The arithmetic said that was not the question.

The lower priorities are covered by any plausible sale price. The middle band is covered at a stretch. The largest claim in the queue sits far enough down the ladder that it needs proceeds well beyond anything the process is likely to produce, which makes it an option on an outcome nobody expects rather than a position.

Three routes existed that were not the queue at all. Buy a senior position from a holder with a reason to sell. Co-invest in a buyer group, which needs a relationship with the process rather than a place in the line. Or wait for a licence expiry that would improve price discovery on the deep positions.

So the four-week question became which of a handful of holders would sell, at what price, and which of three structures suited the fund. That is answerable. The original was not.

A separate client, holding unpaid arbitration awards and no operations in the country, faced the mirror image. Two competitors announced an asset swap and the client wanted to know whether a transaction it was not part of changed what its claims were worth.

It did, three ways. The consolidation raised the barrier to any operational return, which made settlement more likely than re-entry. The transition government's appetite for new operators created a structural reason to clear legacy claims, because unresolved awards create legal risk over the state company's cargoes and receivables and deter exactly the investment it was courting. And the gas side improved the long-run feed outlook for a plant in which the client holds equity.

The claims were leverage, not history. The posture moved from enforcement to structured settlement, timed to the licensing window rather than to the arbitration calendar.

The question a client brings is rarely the question that can be answered. Most of the work is the rewrite.
