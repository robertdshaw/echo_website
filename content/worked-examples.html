A distressed credit fund, Venezuelan sovereign and state oil paper

The month the families
started leaving

A carrier group off the coast, a capital that had cried wolf before, and a client holding paper that would be worth very different things depending on who ran the country in March.

Ten weeks of monitoring, mid-October to early January. Delivered through a London-based advisory partner.

The question the client brought was not whether the government would survive. It was who would be on the other side of a contract in six months, and whether that person would recognise the paper.

Through the second half of November the same pattern kept arriving from sources that do not usually agree and were not talking to each other. Families of senior officials leaving through the western border. Ministries destroying records. Senior figures preparing fallback positions in the south. None of it would have cleared our bar alone. Together, from independent channels, it did.

What mattered was the gap between the readings. External pressure was climbing and jumped again in the last week of November. Elite cohesion had been falling all autumn and turned up off its low at the start of December. The military reading went the other way entirely and sat at its lowest point on the day we made the call.

The judgement was that the military line could not hold against the other two, and that when it went it would go quickly. That is why the call was a rupture rather than a slow decline.

The signs were on the table ninety days out. We made the final call thirty two days before it happened. The president was taken, the successor came from inside the ruling coalition, the armed forces stayed intact, and the client's contract and counterparty assumptions held.

The questions were written about outcomes rather than about mechanisms, which is why they settled on the date whichever way it came. That is a design choice, not luck.

Consensus in the press was collapse or the opposition taking power. The client positioned for succession inside the existing structure.

A US oil major, and the plant where its cargo loads

The train was hit.
Proving it took three days

Two drones came in over a Gulf industrial city on a March morning. The wires said an energy facility had been struck. The company had product in that complex and needed to know which part.

Rapid assessment, March 2026. Seventy two hours from the first report to a sourced answer.

An energy facility covers a switchyard, a storage tank, an office block and a liquefaction train equally well. The distance between the first and the last is the distance between a news item and a supply interruption, and nothing in the early reporting closed it.

Our joint venture partner had people on the ground who said a train had been damaged. Human reporting is the fastest thing available and the hardest to act on by itself. The person telling you is rarely positioned to see the whole plant, and in a week like that one, everybody on site has heard something.

So we went after the things that cannot be talked into existence. Thermal imagery showed where heat had stopped being produced and where it had not. Successive satellite passes showed the physical state of the structure rather than the claim about it. AIS tracking showed which carriers berthed, which loaded and which left, and on what schedule, because a train that is down eventually shows up as a ship that does not sail.

The three agreed with each other and with what the partner’s people had said. Within seventy two hours of the first report the company had the train identified, the damage established, and a line of sourcing written beside each claim rather than a single confident sentence.

That was the difference that mattered. The company was not deciding whether to believe a rumour. It was deciding what to do about a fact it could show to its own board, and later to anyone who asked how it had known.

Human reporting told us where to look. It was the imagery and the ships that made it something the company could act on.
